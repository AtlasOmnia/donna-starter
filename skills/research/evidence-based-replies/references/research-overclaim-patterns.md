# Research overclaim pattern: persona-file debate

## Example pattern
A public discussion compared:
- an online exchange about long versus short agent persona files
- a research paper on persona-augmented benchmarking
- a public repository containing a verbose persona example

The correction needed was not more summary. It was to address the **misunderstanding of the research**.

## Source split
### What the paper supported
- LLM performance can vary when prompts are rewritten in different personas / writing styles.
- Style sensitivity can affect benchmark outcomes even when semantic content is preserved.

### What the paper did **not** support
- Longer persona files are better.
- More literary prose improves agent performance.
- A narrative character bible is superior to a compressed operational soul.
- "Persona matters" therefore "this specific verbose Jarvis implementation is better."

## Useful reply frame
Use when someone cites research too broadly:

> The misunderstanding here is that the paper is being used to support a stronger claim than it actually makes.
>
> What the research shows is ___.
>
> What it does **not** show is ___.
>
> That is the leap I’m pushing back on.

## Applied distinction
- The verbose persona example argued for consistency, identity, tone, conviction, and hard stops.
- That is design doctrine, not empirical proof that a longer prose-heavy persona is better prompt engineering.
- A concise behavioral specification can be a better control artifact for always-loaded agent context.
- Best conclusion: the paper supports persona sensitivity, but not verbosity or narrative sprawl.

## Reusable lesson
When the user asks for a reply grounded in research, do not stop at "both views have merit." Explicitly separate:
1. the paper's finding
2. the claimed implication
3. the unsupported implementation leap
